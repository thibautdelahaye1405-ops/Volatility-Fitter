\section{Pricing on the lattice}
\label{sec:lvlattice}

\subsection{The implicit march and its M-matrix}

The forward equation is solved on a strike grid over $[0,y_{\max}]$ with
Dirichlet boundaries $c(\cdot,0)=1$ and $c(\cdot,y_{\max})=0$, by
\emph{fully implicit} Euler steps, the field evaluated at the new time
level.  On a possibly non-uniform grid with gaps
$\Delta y_i^-=y_i-y_{i-1}$ and $\Delta y_i^+=y_{i+1}-y_i$, the discrete
generator $\mathcal L$ encoding $\tfrac12 v y^{2}\partial_{yy}$ has the
three-point stencil
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{i,i\mp1}
=\frac{v_i\,y_i^{2}}{(\Delta y_i^-+\Delta y_i^+)\,\Delta y_i^{\mp}}\;\ge0,
\qquad
\mathcal L_{ii}=-\big(\mathcal L_{i,i-1}+\mathcal L_{i,i+1}\big),
\label{eq:lvstencil}
\end{equation}
and each step solves one tridiagonal system,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal M^{n+1}U^{n+1}=U^{n}+(\text{boundary terms}),
\qquad
\mathcal M^{n+1}=I-\Delta\tau\,\mathcal L^{n+1}.
\label{eq:lvstep}
\end{equation}
Now the promised discipline: does this discretization \emph{inherit}
\cref{prop:lvpropagate}, or merely approximate it?  The answer runs through
one classical linear-algebra fact, and it is worth seeing how little is
needed.

\begin{proposition}[The step matrix is an M-matrix]
\label{prop:lvmmatrix}
For any $v\ge0$ and any $\Delta\tau>0$, the matrix
$\mathcal M=I-\Delta\tau\mathcal L$ of \eqref{eq:lvstep} has positive
diagonal, non-positive off-diagonals, and each diagonal entry exceeds the
absolute sum of its row's off-diagonals by at least $1$ (exactly $1$ at
fully interior rows).  Hence $\mathcal M$ is a strictly diagonally
dominant nonsingular M-matrix, and $\mathcal M^{-1}\ge0$ elementwise.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Read the sign pattern off \eqref{eq:lvstencil}: the off-diagonals of
$\mathcal M$ are $-\Delta\tau\mathcal L_{i,i\mp1}\le0$, the diagonal is
$1+\Delta\tau(\mathcal L_{i,i-1}+\mathcal L_{i,i+1})\ge1$, and the
dominance gap is the $1$ of the identity at interior rows, more where a
stencil arm reaches a boundary column.  This is the defining pattern of
a nonsingular M-matrix, and M-matrices are inverse-positive
\citep{BermanPlemmons1994}.  (Concretely: Jacobi iteration for
$\mathcal M u=b$ with $b\ge0$ keeps nonnegative iterates and converges by
dominance, so $u\ge0$.)
\end{proof}

$\mathcal M^{-1}\ge0$ is the discrete ghost of the heat kernel being
positive, and everything follows from it:

\begin{corollary}[Discrete maximum principle, unconditionally]
\label{cor:lvdmp}
For any $\Delta\tau>0$ the scheme \eqref{eq:lvstep} is \emph{monotone} ---
$U^{n}\ge V^{n}$ with the same boundary data implies $U^{n+1}\ge V^{n+1}$
--- and every step keeps the values between the extremes of the previous
step and the boundary data: the march is unconditionally
$\ell^{\infty}$-stable, with no step-size restriction, and cannot create
new extrema.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Monotonicity is $\mathcal M^{-1}\ge0$ applied to $U^{n}-V^{n}\ge0$.  For
the upper bound, let $M_\ast$ be the larger of $\max_iU^{n}_i$ and the
boundary values: because the interior rows of $\mathcal M$ sum to $1$ once
the boundary columns are included, the constant vector obeys
$\mathcal M(M_\ast\1)\ge M_\ast\1$ against the right-hand side of
\eqref{eq:lvstep}, and inverse positivity turns that into
$U^{n+1}\le M_\ast\1$.  The lower bound is symmetric.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What the M-matrix argument does and does not prove]
\label{rem:lvscope}
\Cref{prop:lvmmatrix,cor:lvdmp} control the call \emph{values}: order
preservation, boundedness, no invented extrema.  They do not by themselves
give nonnegative second differences --- a bounded monotone vector can
still be locally concave --- so ``the scheme cannot manufacture negative
densities'' would require a separate convexity-preservation proof for the
actual stencil and boundary treatment, which this chapter does not claim.
What can be claimed is what the reference implementation does: the scheme
is monotone and stable by theorem, and the butterfly and calendar
cleanliness of the \emph{output} is measured on the price grid with every
fit.  \Cref{sec:lvexamples} reports the measured minima for the chapter's
two calibrated sheets; they sit at the scale of solver rounding.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Why not Crank--Nicolson}

First-order accuracy in time looks like a price worth negotiating, and
Crank--Nicolson --- replace \eqref{eq:lvstep} by
$(I-\tfrac{\Delta\tau}{2}\mathcal L)U^{n+1}
=(I+\tfrac{\Delta\tau}{2}\mathcal L)U^{n}$ --- is the standard
second-order offer.  Look at what it costs.  The implicit factor is still
an M-matrix, but monotonicity now also needs the \emph{explicit} factor to
be nonnegative, and its diagonal is
$1-\tfrac{\Delta\tau}{2}(\mathcal L_{i,i-1}+\mathcal L_{i,i+1})$:
nonnegative only under the CFL-like bound
\begin{equation}
\Delta\tau\;\le\;\frac{2}{\mathcal L_{i,i-1}+\mathcal L_{i,i+1}}
\;=\;\frac{2\,\Delta y^{2}}{v_i\,y_i^{2}}
\quad\text{(uniform grid)}.
\label{eq:lvcfl}
\end{equation}
Refining the strike grid --- which \cref{sec:lvinfo} will show short-dated
smiles \emph{require} --- tightens this bound quadratically.  At a $40\%$
local volatility on a $\Delta y=0.005$ lattice the bound is
$\MacLvLatCflBound$ years, more than thirty times smaller than the
standard $0.01$ marching step: monotone Crank--Nicolson on realistic
lattices is simply not on offer.  When the bound is violated the scheme
remains second-order \emph{accurate} but is no longer \emph{monotone}, and
the payoff kink at $y=1$ excites the classic oscillation.
\Cref{fig:lvmonotone} shows what that means for the object we care about:
the discrete density.  Damped start-up steps (a few implicit steps before
switching to Crank--Nicolson) are the standard repair
\citep{GilesCarter2006}; the reference implementation's default declines
the negotiation altogether and trades one order of time accuracy for
\cref{cor:lvdmp}.

\subsection{Reading out, and the operator's blind spot}

The observation operator is plain: the normalized call at a quote's
$(\tau,y)$ is read off the marched solution by interpolation, at maturities
that are forced to be lattice time-nodes.  One subtlety deserves a
principle of its own, because it is general to inverse problems.  An
optimizer facing a \emph{fixed} discretization will happily bend its
parameters to cancel the operator's own discretization error, so the
in-operator residual systematically flatters the fit --- validating a
reconstruction with the operator that produced it is the inverse-problems
``inverse crime''.  The chapter's protocol therefore reprices every fit on
a \emph{refined} operator (half the strike step, four times the time
steps) and reports both numbers.  The gap between them is not noise; it is
the measured size of the compensation, and \cref{sec:lvexamples} will show
it is the dominant error term at the short end.
