\section{The equation the surface must obey}
\label{sec:lveq}

\subsection{Forward: from the field to every marginal}

Fix a bounded measurable field $v\ge0$ and let $Y$ solve
\eqref{eq:lvsde}.  We now derive the equation that the normalized call
satisfies as a function of maturity.  It is the computational engine of
the whole chapter: solve it once, marching left to right in maturity,
and every expiry's smile comes out along the way.

\begin{proposition}[The forward Dupire equation]
\label{prop:lvforward}
Suppose $Y_\tau$ has a density $f(\tau,\cdot)$ such that
$v y^{2}f$ and $y\,\partial_y(v y^{2}f)$ are integrable and vanish at
$y=0$ and as $y\to\infty$.
Then the normalized call $c(\tau,y)=\E[(Y_\tau-y)^+]$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\partial_\tau c(\tau,y)
=\tfrac12\,v(\tau,y)\,y^{2}\,\partial_{yy}c(\tau,y),
\qquad c(0,y)=(1-y)^{+}.\;}
\label{eq:lvdupire}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The proof has three steps: find the equation the density obeys,
integrate that equation against the call payoff, and move the
derivatives across.

\emph{Step 1: the density.}  Apply It\^o's formula to $\Xi(Y_\tau)$,
for a smooth function $\Xi$ --- recall that It\^o adds a second-order
term to the ordinary chain rule, because the squared increment
$(\dd Y)^2$ accumulates at the rate $v\,Y^{2}\,\dd\tau$ here:
\[
\dd\,\Xi(Y_\tau)
=\Xi'(Y_\tau)\,\sqrt{v}\,Y_\tau\,\dd W_\tau
+\tfrac12\,v(\tau,Y_\tau)\,Y_\tau^{2}\,\Xi''(Y_\tau)\,\dd\tau .
\]
Take expectations.  The $\dd W$ term has mean zero, and writing the
remaining expectations against the density $f$,
\[
\frac{\dd}{\dd\tau}\int_0^\infty \Xi(y)\,f(\tau,y)\,\dd y
=\tfrac12\int_0^\infty v(\tau,y)\,y^{2}\,\Xi''(y)\,f(\tau,y)\,\dd y .
\]
Let $\Xi$ vanish outside a bounded set away from $0$, and integrate
the right side by parts twice, moving both derivatives off $\Xi$
and onto $v y^{2}f$ (no boundary terms, since $\Xi$ vanishes near
the ends):
\[
\int v\,y^{2}f\,\Xi''\,\dd y
=-\int \partial_y\big(v\,y^{2}f\big)\,\Xi'\,\dd y
=\int \partial_{yy}\big(v\,y^{2}f\big)\,\Xi\,\dd y .
\]
This holds for every such $\Xi$; and if the two integrands differed
anywhere, choosing a $\Xi$ concentrated near that point would break
the equality.  So they agree:
\[
\partial_\tau f=\tfrac12\,\partial_{yy}\big(v\,y^{2}f\big).
\]
This is the \emph{Fokker--Planck equation} --- the equation every
diffusion's density obeys; the two displays above are its entire
derivation.

\emph{Step 2: integrate against the payoff.}  The call is
$c(\tau,y_0)=\int_0^\infty(y-y_0)^{+}f(\tau,y)\,\dd y$; differentiating
under the integral sign (which the decay hypothesis licenses),
\[
\partial_\tau c(\tau,y_0)
=\int_0^\infty (y-y_0)^{+}\,\partial_\tau f(\tau,y)\,\dd y
=\tfrac12\int_0^\infty (y-y_0)^{+}\,
  \partial_{yy}\big(v\,y^{2}f\big)\,\dd y .
\]

\emph{Step 3: move the derivatives onto the payoff.}  Integrate by
parts once.  The payoff's derivative in $y$ is the step function
$\1_{\{y>y_0\}}$ --- slope $0$ below the strike, $1$ above --- and the
boundary term $(y-y_0)^{+}\,\partial_y(vy^{2}f)$ vanishes at both ends
by the decay hypothesis:
\[
\tfrac12\int_0^\infty (y-y_0)^{+}\,\partial_{yy}\big(v y^{2}f\big)\,\dd y
=-\tfrac12\int_{y_0}^\infty \partial_y\big(v\,y^{2}f\big)\,\dd y .
\]
The remaining integral is elementary: it integrates an exact derivative,
and $v y^{2}f$ vanishes at the far end, so
\[
-\tfrac12\int_{y_0}^\infty \partial_y\big(v\,y^{2}f\big)\,\dd y
=-\tfrac12\Big[v\,y^{2}f\Big]_{y_0}^{\infty}
=\tfrac12\,v(\tau,y_0)\,y_0^{2}\,f(\tau,y_0).
\]
Finally, recall the density identity of \cref{sec:intro}:
differentiating the call twice in strike returns the density,
$f(\tau,y_0)=\partial_{yy}c(\tau,y_0)$.  Substituting gives
\eqref{eq:lvdupire}.  The initial condition is $Y_0=1$, whose call is
$(1-y)^{+}$.
\end{proof}

Read \eqref{eq:lvdupire} as a physics problem.  It is a heat equation
in the \emph{strike} variable: the call price plays the temperature,
the payoff $(1-y)^{+}$ is the initial temperature profile, maturity
plays the role of time, and $\tfrac12 v y^{2}$ is the conductivity of
the material at position $y$ and time $\tau$.  One field, one linear
march, and every marginal law of the surface comes out at once.  The
analogy is not decoration; it is the source of every structural
property this chapter uses.  Heat flow spreads bumps and never sharpens
them, and ``spreading'', translated into option language, is exactly
the no-arbitrage direction.  The next proposition makes that precise,
and it is the reason this chapter can \emph{promise} validity rather
than police it.  (One name in it deserves its reminder: a
\emph{butterfly} is the three-strike spread whose payoff is a tent, and
its price is a second difference of call prices --- so ruling out
negative butterfly prices is exactly the condition
$\partial_{yy}c\ge0$, a nonnegative implied density.)

\begin{proposition}[Positive local variance propagates no-arbitrage]
\label{prop:lvpropagate}
Let $c$ solve \eqref{eq:lvdupire} with $v\ge0$ bounded.  Then for every
$\tau>0$: (i) $\partial_{yy}c(\tau,\cdot)\ge0$ --- each slice is
butterfly-free; (ii) $\partial_\tau c(\tau,y)\ge0$ --- the normalized
call is non-decreasing in maturity at fixed $y$, which is the calendar
order of \cref{sec:calendar}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Differentiate \eqref{eq:lvdupire} twice in $y$: the function
$f=\partial_{yy}c$ --- by Step~3's density identity, the same $f$ as
in \cref{prop:lvforward} --- satisfies
$\partial_\tau f=\tfrac12\,\partial_{yy}(v y^{2}f)$ --- the
Fokker--Planck equation of Step~1 again --- with initial datum
$\partial_{yy}(1-y)^{+}$, the unit point mass at $y=1$: at $\tau=0$
all the probability sits at $Y=1$.  Equations of this type preserve
nonnegativity.  Probabilistically there is nothing to prove:
$f(\tau,\cdot)$ \emph{is} the density of the diffusion
\eqref{eq:lvsde} started at $1$, and densities are nonnegative.
Analytically, the same fact is the parabolic maximum principle,
which we quote from PDE theory.  With $f\ge0$ established, part~(ii)
is the equation \eqref{eq:lvdupire} itself read as a sign statement:
$\partial_\tau c=\tfrac12 v y^{2}f\ge0$.
\end{proof}

Pause on what this proposition buys, because it is the strategy of the
entire chapter.  In Chapters~2 and~3, validity was work, done slice by
slice: Chapter~2's LQD chart (its log-quantile-density coordinates, in
which every parameter choice is automatically a law) made butterfly
freedom an identity within one
expiry, the volatility chart had to police $g_{\rm D}\ge0$, and
calendar order across expiries was in both cases a separate condition.
Here, one sign condition on one function of two variables delivers both
families of conditions, across the whole surface, automatically.  The
rest of the chapter is organized around protecting that gain.
Choose a parametrization in which $v>0$ is trivial to enforce
(\cref{sec:lvsheet}).  Choose a discretization that \emph{inherits} the
proposition on a finite grid, rather than merely approximating it
(\cref{sec:lvlattice}).  Then measure what little the finite grid
leaks, instead of assuming it leaks nothing (\cref{sec:lvexamples}).

\subsection{Backward: from the surface to the field}

We now know how a field produces a surface.  Read the equation the
other way.  If the surface is given --- so that both derivatives of $c$
are known --- then \eqref{eq:lvdupire} can simply be solved for the
coefficient:
\begin{equation}
v(\tau,y)=\frac{\partial_\tau c(\tau,y)}
{\tfrac12\,y^{2}\,\partial_{yy}c(\tau,y)},
\label{eq:lvextract}
\end{equation}
wherever the density in the denominator is positive.  This is the
uniqueness half of the correspondence: within the class
\eqref{eq:lvsde}, at most one field is consistent with a given surface,
and \eqref{eq:lvextract} computes it pointwise.  The formula becomes
most instructive when translated into the coordinates of Chapter~3 ---
the volatility chart, where a surface is the total-variance function
$w(k,\tau)$.

\begin{proposition}[The field in the volatility chart]
\label{prop:lvratio}
Write the surface in implied form, $c=\Black(k,w(k,\tau))$ with
$w(\cdot,\tau)$ the total-variance slice.  Then \eqref{eq:lvextract}
reads
\begin{equation}
v(\tau,e^{k})=\frac{\partial_\tau w(k,\tau)}{g_{\rm D}(k)},
\label{eq:lvratio}
\end{equation}
with $g_{\rm D}$ the Durrleman factor of \eqref{eq:durrleman},
evaluated on the slice at maturity $\tau$ \citep{Gatheral2006}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Compute the numerator and the denominator of \eqref{eq:lvextract} in
turn.

\emph{Numerator.}  At fixed $k$, the call is the Black function of the
slice's total variance, so by the chain rule
$\partial_\tau c=\partial_w\Black\cdot\partial_\tau w$.  The
derivative $\partial_w\Black$ is the vega of the Black formula in
total-variance units --- the sensitivity of the price to the variance
the formula is fed --- and it is worth computing once in full.  The
normalized call is $\Black=\PhiN(d_+)-e^{k}\PhiN(d_-)$ with
$d_\pm=-k/\sqrt w\pm\sqrt w/2$.  The two arguments satisfy
\[
d_+-d_-=\sqrt w,\qquad d_++d_-=-\frac{2k}{\sqrt w},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
d_+^{2}-d_-^{2}=(d_++d_-)(d_+-d_-)=-2k .
\]
Because $\phin(d)=e^{-d^{2}/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$, the ratio of the two
normal densities is
\[
\frac{\phin(d_+)}{\phin(d_-)}
=\exp\!\Big(\!-\tfrac{d_+^{2}-d_-^{2}}{2}\Big)=e^{k},
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \phin(d_+)=e^{k}\phin(d_-).
\]
Now differentiate $\Black$ in $w$ and use this identity:
\[
\partial_w\Black
=\phin(d_+)\,\partial_w d_+-e^{k}\phin(d_-)\,\partial_w d_-
=\phin(d_+)\,\partial_w(d_+-d_-)
=\phin(d_+)\,\partial_w\sqrt w
=\frac{e^{k}\phin(d_-)}{2\sqrt w}.
\]

\emph{Denominator.}  Two facts convert $\partial_{yy}c$ into chart
language.  First, the density of $Y$ transforms into the density of
the log return $X=\log Y$ by the usual change of variables: with
$y=e^{k}$, $f(\tau,y)=f_X(k)/y$ (the factor $1/y$ is
$\dd y=y\,\dd k$).  Second --- this is \eqref{eq:durrleman}, quoted
from Chapter~2 --- the log-return density of a slice with total
variance $w$ is
\[
f_X(k)=g_{\rm D}(k)\,\frac{\phin(d_-)}{\sqrt w},
\]
where $g_{\rm D}$ is the Durrleman function: up to the positive factor
$\phin(d_-)/\sqrt w$, it \emph{is} the probability density the smile
implies, so $g_{\rm D}\ge0$ is exactly the butterfly condition
Chapter~3 policed.  The denominator of \eqref{eq:lvextract} is then
\[
\tfrac12\,y^{2}\,\partial_{yy}c
=\tfrac12\,y^{2}\,f(\tau,y)
=\tfrac12\,y\,f_X(k)
=\tfrac12\,e^{k}\,g_{\rm D}(k)\,\frac{\phin(d_-)}{\sqrt w}.
\]
Divide numerator by denominator: the factors $e^{k}$, $\phin(d_-)$ and
$2\sqrt w$ all cancel, leaving
$v=\partial_\tau w/g_{\rm D}$.
\end{proof}

The reader has met both halves of this ratio before, as the two
families of static validity.  The numerator is the calendar increment:
it is nonnegative exactly when the slices around maturity $\tau$ are
calendar-ordered in the sense of \cref{sec:calendar}.  The denominator
is the butterfly factor: it is nonnegative exactly when the slice at
$\tau$ implies a genuine (nonnegative) density.  So \eqref{eq:lvratio}
is more than a formula; it is a compatibility statement.  \emph{A
local-volatility field exists precisely where the surface is valid},
and each mode of static arbitrage has its own place to fail: a
calendar violation makes the numerator negative, a butterfly violation
the denominator.  This chapter therefore takes as its input hypothesis
what Chapters~2--3 produced as output: slices that are butterfly-free
and calendar-ordered, in the normalized units of \cref{sec:calendar}.
Where the hypothesis fails, no field exists, and \eqref{eq:lvratio}
says so by changing sign.

\Cref{fig:lvratio} evaluates the ratio on the book's frozen SPY
surface --- the snapshot's eight fitted slices, with total variance
interpolated linearly in $\tau$ between expiries.  Walk its three
panels left to right.  Panel~(a) is the numerator: the calendar
increment $\partial_\tau w$, piecewise constant in $\tau$ because the
interpolation between expiries is linear, and largest toward the
short-dated put side, where the smile grows fastest with maturity.
Panel~(b) is the denominator: each slice's Durrleman factor, the
implied-density factor of the chart.  Panel~(c) is their ratio,
displayed as local volatility $\sqrt v$: a steep put-side wall, a
quiet call side, sharp structure at the short end --- the field
inherits the smile's features in recognizable form, and where the data
speaks the extracted values range from \MacLvRatioLocMinPct\% to
\MacLvRatioLocMaxPct\%.  The grey cells are the diagnostic part of the
picture: there one of the two signs fails and no local variance
exists.  They amount to \MacLvRatioBadSharePct\% of the displayed
mesh, and every one of them sits \emph{outside} the common quoted
support of the two expiries that bracket it --- restricted to the
quoted region the share is \MacLvRatioBadQuotedSharePct\% --- in
territory owned by extrapolation conventions rather than by prices.

One caution closes the section.  The surface in this figure is fitted
and smooth, and evaluating the ratio on a fitted surface is a
legitimate operation --- it is how the figure was drawn.  What must
never be done is to apply the formula to the market's raw quotes.  The
next section shows why.
