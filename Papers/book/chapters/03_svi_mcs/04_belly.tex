\section{What the inequalities do not check: the belly}
\label{sec:svibelly}

The floor condition of \cref{tab:tiers} guards the vertex, and the cap
\eqref{eq:leecap} guards the wings.  This section shows that the region
between them --- the belly of the smile --- is guarded by neither, on a
concrete slice that passes every cheap test and still fails to be a
law.

The root of the problem is that the word ``convex'' hides two different
second derivatives.  The family \eqref{eq:svi} has $w''>0$ by
construction: the \emph{variance curve} is convex in log-moneyness.
What no-arbitrage requires is different: the \emph{option price} must be
convex in the strike.  A butterfly spread --- buy one option at
$K-\delta$, sell two at $K$, buy one at $K+\delta$ --- has a nonnegative
payoff, so its price must be nonnegative, and that price is (up to
scale) the second difference of the call price in strike.  Price
convexity is what the sign of $g_{\rm D}$ tests, and $g_{\rm D}$ mixes
$w$, $w'$, and $w''$.  In the wings the two notions of convexity agree
--- \cref{prop:gtail} showed that the sign of $g_{\rm D}$ out there is
decided by the wing slope.  In the interior they need not agree.

That the gap is real, and not a defect of some particular fitting code,
is shown by a classical example due to Vogt, reproduced as Example~3.1
by \citet{GatheralJacquier2014}:
\[
(a_S,b_S,\rho_S,m_S,s_S)=(-0.0410,\ 0.1331,\ 0.3060,\ 0.3586,\ 0.4153).
\]
Score this slice against \cref{tab:tiers}, row by row.  The structural
conditions hold: $b_S>0$, $|\rho_S|<1$, $s_S>0$.  The floor holds: the
minimum total variance, from \eqref{eq:svivertex}, is
$w^*=\MacSviVogtWmin$, safely positive.  The wings hold with an
enormous margin: the larger slope is
$\max(\beta_L,\beta_R)=\MacSviVogtLee$, an order of magnitude below the
cap, and by \eqref{eq:gtail} both tails of $g_{\rm D}$ settle near
$\MacSviVogtTail$ --- clean, positive tail density on both sides.
Everywhere the cheap inequalities look, this slice is impeccable.

Yet it is not a law.  In the interior, $g_{\rm D}$ dips to
$\MacSviVogtGmin$ near $k=\MacSviVogtKdip$ --- squarely between the two
clean tails.  The implied density is negative there, which means the
slice prices some butterfly below zero.  \Cref{fig:svivogt} shows both
halves of the verdict.  In panel~(a), the total-variance curve itself:
smooth, convex, floor marked, nothing visibly wrong --- this is the
point; the defect is invisible in the chart everyone looks at.  In
panel~(b), the same slice's Durrleman function: it approaches the
positive limit \eqref{eq:gtail} on both sides, exactly as the wing
analysis promises, and turns negative in between.  The wing conditions
fence the tails; the belly escapes them.

Could a better inequality close the gap?  In principle, yes: the exact
butterfly-free domain of the five SVI parameters has been characterized
completely by \citet{MartiniMingone2022}.  But the characterization is
not one short inequality --- it involves a parameter rescaling, root
finding, and a numerical minimization --- and it is specific to this
five-parameter family, with no analogue for the superposition family of
\cref{sec:mcs}.  A family-by-family exact domain is not a strategy a
multi-family fitter can build on.

The reference implementation adopts a different division of labor for
every family in the volatility chart, and it is the chapter's second
convention.  \emph{During} the fit, the floor and the cap are enforced
inside the least-squares residual --- the stacked vector of per-quote
misfits that the optimizer drives toward zero --- by appending two
cheap penalty rows to it:
\begin{equation}
W\,\max\!\big(-(a_S+b_Ss_Sq_S),\,0\big),
\qquad
W\,\max\!\big(b_S(1+|\rho_S|)-\beta_{\max},\,0\big),
\label{eq:svipenalties}
\end{equation}
with $W$ a multiplier large enough to dominate any violation.  Read the
two rows: the first is active only when the floor $w^*=a_S+b_Ss_Sq_S$
of \eqref{eq:svivertex} goes negative, the second only when the larger
wing $b_S(1+|\rho_S|)$ exceeds the cap.  A row of this shape is called
a \emph{hinge}: it is exactly zero whenever its inequality holds, so a
clean fit is identical with or without it, and it only pushes back when
the optimizer strays.  \emph{After} the fit, the condition the hinges
cannot see is checked where it lives: $g_{\rm D}$ is evaluated on a
dense grid of strikes across the traded range --- always from the
model's own derivatives \eqref{eq:svideriv}, never by differencing
prices, which would inject curvature noise at exactly the scale being
measured --- and the slice is accepted only if the sampled minimum
clears a small tolerance.  This is the same posture as the numerical
certificates of \cref{sec:certificates}: an analytic guarantee where
one exists, and where it does not, a computed check on the finished
object, with its scope (the sampled grid on the traded range, not the
whole line) stated rather than implied.  The minimum of $g_{\rm D}$
over that grid is called the fit's \emph{validity margin}; the
comparison of \cref{sec:comparison} reports it for every fit of every
family.

One could try to push the butterfly condition into the optimizer too,
as a hinge on sampled values of $g_{\rm D}$ over a grid --- and the
superposition family of \cref{sec:mcs}, whose localized bump
corrections (its \emph{kernels}, built there) can bend the density in
ways a hyperbola cannot, carries exactly such a row
(\cref{app:protocol}).  It helps, but it settles nothing: a
finite-weight hinge is traded against the data term like any other
residual row, so it discourages violations without excluding them, and
\cref{sec:comparison} shows fits that fail the final check with that
hinge active.  In the other direction, trusting the cheap fences alone
would publish the Vogt slice.  So the division stands: fence cheaply
during the fit to keep the optimizer in the region where the check
usually passes --- and let the check decide.
