\section{One hyperbola}
\label{sec:svigeom}

Everything in the next three sections rests on closed-form facts about
\eqref{eq:svi}.  Write
\begin{equation}
x_S=k-m_S,\qquad r_S=\sqrt{x_S^2+s_S^2},\qquad q_S=\sqrt{1-\rho_S^2}
\label{eq:svishort}
\end{equation}
(the subscripts keep the ledger's $x$ and the moment order $r$ of
\eqref{eq:lee} untouched).

\begin{proposition}[Geometry of one slice]
\label{prop:svigeom}
Under $b_S>0$, $|\rho_S|<1$, $s_S>0$, the slice \eqref{eq:svi} is strictly
convex with a single minimum:
\begin{align}
w'(k)&=b_S\Big(\rho_S+\frac{x_S}{r_S}\Big), &
w''(k)&=\frac{b_S\,s_S^2}{r_S^3}>0,
\label{eq:svideriv}\\
k^*&=m_S-\frac{s_S\rho_S}{q_S}, &
w^*&=w(k^*)=a_S+b_S\,s_S\,q_S .
\label{eq:svivertex}
\end{align}
Its far field is two straight rays:
\begin{equation}
w(k)=\beta_L|k|+O(1)\ (k\to-\infty),\qquad
w(k)=\beta_R\,k+O(1)\ (k\to+\infty),
\label{eq:sviwings}
\end{equation}
with
\[
\beta_L=b_S(1-\rho_S),\qquad \beta_R=b_S(1+\rho_S).
\]
The curvature at the vertex is
$\kappa^*=w''(k^*)=b_S\,q_S^{3}/s_S$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:svi} gives \eqref{eq:svideriv}; $b_S,s_S>0$ make
$w''>0$ everywhere, so any stationary point is the unique global minimum.
Setting $w'=0$ gives $x_S/r_S=-\rho_S$, hence
$x_S^2(1-\rho_S^2)=\rho_S^2s_S^2$ with $x_S$ of the opposite sign to
$\rho_S$, i.e.\ $x_S=-s_S\rho_S/q_S$ and $r_S=s_S/q_S$; substituting
yields \eqref{eq:svivertex} and $\kappa^*=b_Ss_S^2(q_S/s_S)^3$.  In either
wing $r_S=|x_S|+O(1/|x_S|)$, which gives \eqref{eq:sviwings}.
\end{proof}

The formula reads as a rounded hinge.  Far from the vertex the hinge has
forgotten its rounding and shows two straight rays, of slope $\beta_L$ on
the left and $\beta_R$ on the right; the tilt $\rho_S$ divides a fixed
total steepness $\beta_L+\beta_R=2b_S$ between the two sides, with
$\rho_S<0$ --- the equity put skew --- making the left ray steeper.  Near
the vertex, $s_S$ decides how sharply one ray turns into the other, $m_S$
places the turn, and $a_S$ raises the whole curve.  \Cref{fig:svizoo}
sweeps each role in isolation.

Two consequences of \cref{prop:svigeom} organize the rest of the chapter.

First, the tails collapse to two numbers.  As $|k|$ grows, $w(k)/|k|$
converges to $\beta_L$ on the put side and $\beta_R$ on the call side, and
no other feature of the slice survives to infinity.  Whatever the market's
tails are worth, this family records them in $(\beta_L,\beta_R)$ alone ---
which is precisely the pair that Lee's moment bound constrains, and
\cref{sec:svilee} develops that correspondence.

Second, strict convexity is a rigidity.  One hyperbola has one belly and
one monotone turn of the slope, from $-\beta_L$ up to $+\beta_R$, and this
excludes a shape that markets do produce.

\begin{proposition}[A convex slice cannot carry a W]
\label{prop:convexww}
A convex function on an interval has no interior strict local maximum.
Hence a total-variance curve with a central trough flanked by two shoulders
--- an interior local maximum on each side --- is not representable by any
globally convex slice, in particular by \eqref{eq:svi}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Convexity makes the one-sided derivatives non-decreasing; a strict interior
maximum would force the derivative to pass from positive to negative,
contradicting monotonicity.  By \cref{prop:svigeom}, $w''>0$ everywhere for
the family \eqref{eq:svi}.
\end{proof}

On a liquid index chain this rigidity is a virtue: it forbids the wiggles
that quote noise would otherwise carve into the curve.  Ahead of a
scheduled event, or in a name with bimodal positioning, it is a ceiling ---
the smile genuinely turns more than once, and the family cannot follow.
\Cref{sec:mcs} removes the ceiling without giving up the wings.
