\section{One hyperbola, five parameters}
\label{sec:svigeom}

We have a formula, \eqref{eq:svi}.  Its five parameters are legible in
a concrete sense: every geometric feature of the curve --- where it
bottoms, how it curves there, how steep each wing is --- comes out in
closed form.

Start by computing, because the formula is friendlier than it looks.
Take
\[
a_S=0.01,\qquad b_S=0.10,\qquad \rho_S=-0.4,\qquad m_S=0,\qquad
s_S=0.2,
\]
with a quarter year of variance time, $\tau=0.25$.  At the money,
$k=0$, the square root is $\sqrt{0^2+0.2^2}=0.2$, so
\[
w(0)=0.01+0.10\,(-0.4\cdot0+0.2)=0.030,
\]
and the implied volatility is $\sqrt{0.030/0.25}=\sqrt{0.120}\approx
34.6\%$.  Further out on the call side, at $k=0.3$: the square root is
$\sqrt{0.09+0.04}\approx0.361$, so
$w(0.3)=0.01+0.10\,(-0.12+0.361)\approx0.0341$, an implied volatility of
about $36.9\%$.  And at $k=-0.3$:
$w(-0.3)=0.01+0.10\,(0.12+0.361)\approx0.0581$, about $48.2\%$.  The
left side came out much higher than the right.  That is the equity put
skew, and it entered through the sign of $\rho_S$: a negative tilt makes
the put side steep and the call side shallow.  Everything else in this
section is this computation done once and for all, in general.

For the general statement, three shorthands keep the algebra readable:
\begin{equation}
x_S=k-m_S,\qquad r_S=\sqrt{x_S^2+s_S^2},\qquad q_S=\sqrt{1-\rho_S^2}
\label{eq:svishort}
\end{equation}
(the subscripts keep Chapter~\ref{ch:lqd}'s transport $x$ and the moment
order $r$ of \eqref{eq:lee} untouched).

\begin{proposition}[Geometry of one slice]
\label{prop:svigeom}
Under $b_S>0$, $|\rho_S|<1$, $s_S>0$, the slice \eqref{eq:svi} is
strictly convex with a single minimum:
\begin{align}
w'(k)&=b_S\Big(\rho_S+\frac{x_S}{r_S}\Big), &
w''(k)&=\frac{b_S\,s_S^2}{r_S^3}>0,
\label{eq:svideriv}\\
k^*&=m_S-\frac{s_S\rho_S}{q_S}, &
w^*&=w(k^*)=a_S+b_S\,s_S\,q_S .
\label{eq:svivertex}
\end{align}
Its far field is two straight rays:
\begin{equation}
w(k)=\beta_L|k|+O(1)\ (k\to-\infty),\qquad
w(k)=\beta_R\,k+O(1)\ (k\to+\infty),
\label{eq:sviwings}
\end{equation}
with
\[
\beta_L=b_S(1-\rho_S),\qquad \beta_R=b_S(1+\rho_S).
\]
The curvature at the vertex is $\kappa^*=w''(k^*)=b_S\,q_S^{3}/s_S$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate \eqref{eq:svi} once and twice in $k$, using
$r_S'=x_S/r_S$:
\[
w'=b_S\Big(\rho_S+\frac{x_S}{r_S}\Big),
\qquad
w''=b_S\,\frac{r_S-x_S\cdot(x_S/r_S)}{r_S^2}
=b_S\,\frac{r_S^2-x_S^2}{r_S^3}
=\frac{b_S\,s_S^2}{r_S^3},
\]
which is \eqref{eq:svideriv}; it is positive because $b_S,s_S>0$, so the
curve is strictly convex and any stationary point is the unique global
minimum.  To find that point, set $w'=0$, i.e.\ $x_S/r_S=-\rho_S$.
Square both sides and use $r_S^2=x_S^2+s_S^2$:
\[
x_S^2=\rho_S^2\,(x_S^2+s_S^2)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x_S^2\,(1-\rho_S^2)=\rho_S^2 s_S^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|x_S|=\frac{s_S|\rho_S|}{q_S}.
\]
Since $r_S>0$, the equation $x_S/r_S=-\rho_S$ forces $x_S$ to have the
sign opposite to $\rho_S$, so $x_S=-s_S\rho_S/q_S$ and hence
$k^*=m_S-s_S\rho_S/q_S$.  At this point
\[
r_S=\sqrt{\frac{s_S^2\rho_S^2}{q_S^2}+s_S^2}
=s_S\,\frac{\sqrt{\rho_S^2+q_S^2}}{q_S}
=\frac{s_S}{q_S},
\]
because $\rho_S^2+q_S^2=1$.  Substituting into \eqref{eq:svi},
\[
w^*=a_S+b_S\Big(-\frac{s_S\rho_S^2}{q_S}+\frac{s_S}{q_S}\Big)
=a_S+b_S\,s_S\,\frac{1-\rho_S^2}{q_S}
=a_S+b_S\,s_S\,q_S,
\]
which is \eqref{eq:svivertex}, and plugging $r_S=s_S/q_S$ into
\eqref{eq:svideriv} gives
$\kappa^*=b_S s_S^2\,(q_S/s_S)^3=b_S q_S^3/s_S$.  Finally, in either
wing $|x_S|\to\infty$ and
$r_S=|x_S|\sqrt{1+s_S^2/x_S^2}=|x_S|+O(1/|x_S|)$; substituting
$r_S\approx|x_S|$ into \eqref{eq:svi} leaves
$w\approx a_S+b_S(\rho_S x_S+|x_S|)$, whose slope in $k$ is
$b_S(\rho_S+1)=\beta_R$ on the right and whose slope in $|k|$ is
$b_S(1-\rho_S)=\beta_L$ on the left --- which is \eqref{eq:sviwings}.
\end{proof}

In words: the curve is a rounded hinge.  Far from the vertex it is two
straight rays, of slope $\beta_L$ on the left and $\beta_R$ on the
right.  The tilt $\rho_S$ divides a fixed total steepness
$\beta_L+\beta_R=2b_S$ between the two sides --- our worked slice had
$\beta_L=0.10\times1.4=0.14$ and $\beta_R=0.06$, steep left, shallow
right.  Near the vertex, $s_S$ decides how sharply one ray turns into
the other, $m_S$ slides the turn left or right, and $a_S$ lifts the
whole curve.  (For the worked slice, \eqref{eq:svivertex} puts the
vertex at $k^*=0-0.2\cdot(-0.4)/\sqrt{0.84}\approx0.087$, with
$w^*=0.01+0.10\cdot0.2\cdot0.917\approx0.028$ --- slightly right of the
money, slightly below $w(0)$, as a left-steep curve requires.)

\Cref{fig:svizoo} sweeps the three shape parameters one at a time around
a common slice, and each panel isolates one of the readings above.  In
panel~(a), only $\rho_S$ varies: watch the left and right rays trade
steepness while their total stays fixed --- the vertex drifts, but the
outer geometry only tilts.  In panel~(b), only $s_S$ varies: the rays do
not move at all, and the curves differ only in how tightly they round
the corner between them.  In panel~(c), only $b_S$ varies: both rays
open or close together, like scissors.  Every member, in every panel, is
one convex curve with one minimum.

We now have the whole geometry.  Two consequences of it organize the
rest of the chapter.

First, the tails collapse to two numbers.  As $|k|$ grows, $w(k)/|k|$
tends to $\beta_L$ on the put side and $\beta_R$ on the call side, and
no other feature of the slice survives to infinity.  Whatever the
market's tails are worth, this family records them in
$(\beta_L,\beta_R)$ alone.  That is exactly the pair that Lee's moment
bound constrains, and \cref{sec:svilee} develops the correspondence.

Second, strict convexity is a rigidity.  One hyperbola has one belly and
one monotone turn of the slope, from $-\beta_L$ up to $+\beta_R$.  This
excludes a shape that markets do produce: the W, a central trough with a
raised shoulder on each side.

\begin{proposition}[A convex slice cannot carry a W]
\label{prop:convexww}
A convex function on an interval has no interior strict local maximum.
Hence a total-variance curve with a central trough flanked by two
shoulders --- an interior local maximum on each side --- is not
representable by any globally convex slice, in particular by
\eqref{eq:svi}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For a convex function the derivative (or, where it fails to exist, the
one-sided derivatives) is non-decreasing.  At a strict interior local
maximum the derivative would have to pass from positive, on the way up,
to negative, on the way down --- a decrease, contradicting monotonicity.
By \cref{prop:svigeom}, $w''>0$ everywhere for the family
\eqref{eq:svi}, so the family is convex and the exclusion applies to it.
\end{proof}

Whether this rigidity helps or hurts depends on the smile in front of
you.  On a liquid index chain it helps: it forbids the wiggles that
quote noise would otherwise carve into the fitted curve.  Ahead of a
scheduled event, or in a single stock (a \emph{name}, in desk speech)
whose holders are split between two scenarios, it is a ceiling --- the
market's smile genuinely turns more than once, and the family cannot
follow it.  \Cref{sec:mcs} removes the ceiling without giving up the
wings.
